How the peg pyramid works

The board is built as a triangle of staggered pegs, with each row one peg wider than the last. A ball dropped at the top has an even chance of moving left or right at each peg, and that choice repeats all the way down. After crossing the full board, the ball lands in one of the bottom pockets, with the number of pockets always one more than the number of rows.

Because every step is a fifty-fifty turn, most balls gather around the centre and only a few travel to the outer edges. Statisticians call this a Galton board, and the shape it creates is a binomial distribution. The payout table mirrors that curve: central pockets usually pay less, while rare edge pockets pay far more.

Multipliers, odds, and the house margin

Each pocket value is set to roughly match its probability, then reduced a little by the house margin. If you multiply the payout by the chance of landing there, the total comes out below one, and that difference is the operator's edge. Most versions publish an RTP somewhere around 97% to 99%, depending on the studio and the chosen settings.

A practical point: no row count or risk level changes the long-run return in the player’s favour. The settings change variance, not the average result.

Six words worth knowing

Pocket
A slot at the base of the board that holds the multiplier for a finished ball.
Rows
The number of peg lines crossed by the ball; more rows mean more pockets and sharper odds.
Risk level
A preset that stretches or compresses the payout table without changing the long-run return much.
House margin
The small gap between true odds and paid odds that supports the operator.
Provably fair
A system of published hashes that lets players confirm the result was not altered.
Variance
The amount of swing around the average outcome, which is what the risk setting mainly controls.

Straight answers to five questions

Is every drop genuinely random?

On regulated platforms, yes. Each turn uses an audited random number generator, and provably fair versions let you check the result after the round.

Do more rows make the game pay better?

No. Extra rows add pockets and stretch the odds curve, so the edge values grow while their chance drops. The overall return stays broadly the same.

Which risk level is the smart choice?

None is mathematically better. Low risk smooths the ride, while high risk puts more value into rare edge hits. The right setting depends on your budget and tolerance for swings.

Can I practise without spending anything?

Yes. The board on this page uses virtual credits, and many operators also run free play versions with the same odds structure.

Does timing or button rhythm influence where a ball lands?

No. The result comes from the random generator, not from how fast you press drop. Any pattern you notice is coincidence, not control.
